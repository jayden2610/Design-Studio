# Monochrome Illustrated Logo System

## Purpose

Create original F&B identities with the focused, editorial ink quality visible in the four rendered studies in `Explorations/`:

1. `fnb-logo-01-bagel-handoff.png`
2. `fnb-logo-01-gimbap-roll.png`
3. `fnb-logo-02-concha-cooling.png`
4. `fnb-logo-02-open-for-bagels.png`

The goal is not to reproduce their food, pose, or composition. Their transferable lesson is **controlled narrative**: one clear food moment, drawn with enough craft to feel human, but with a disciplined hierarchy so the mark still reads at a glance.

## The core rule: one moment, not a whole brand world

An illustrated logo starts with a frozen service or food ritual. It has only three visual roles:

| Role | Job | Limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hero | The thing read first: the food or service object. | One only. It should occupy roughly 50-65% of the illustrated mass. |
| Action | The gesture that makes the mark feel alive. | One hand, one turn, one roll, one lift, or one placement. |
| Context | The single cue that proves this is a business or ritual. | One bag, receipt, rack, awning, mat, counter edge, or sign. |

Texture is not a fourth role. Hatching, dots, crumbs, and labels only support a form that is already clear without them.

If two elements compete to be the hero, remove or subordinate one. If the idea needs more than one action or more than one context cue, split it into separate directions.

## What the four studies teach

| Study | First read | Action | Context cue | What to retain | What to reduce first |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bagel Handoff | Bagel emerging from a paper bag | Hand offers the bag | Receipt and counter line | Service ritual and bag silhouette | Receipt copy, extra hatching, most sleeve detail |
| Gimbap Roll | Dark roll silhouette | Hand rolls or holds it | Bamboo mat | Food shape and rolling motion | Mat grid and surface texture |
| Concha Cooling | One patterned bun | Hand places it down | Cooling rack | Product centerpiece and resting gesture | Rack grid, spare steam, fine crust texture |
| Open for Bagels | Round hanging bagel sign | Hand turns it open | Awning | Opening ritual and sign metaphor | Awning stripes, chain detail, extra motion marks |

Use the first two columns to make the mark recognizable in three seconds. The context cue earns its place only if it clarifies the ritual. Everything else is optional.

## The build flow

### 1. Write a one-sentence moment

Use this exact structure:

```text
A [person/hand/tool] [one action] a [specific food or service object] at/with a [single business cue].
```

Good: `A hand slides a pandan-toast sleeve across a counter.`

Too busy: `A barista carries kopi, toast, a bag, a menu, and a market stall through a street scene.`

The sentence should contain one verb. If it needs `and`, it is usually two directions.

### 2. Choose the silhouette before the details

Draw or prompt the following in this order:

1. the hero object as a solid black-and-white silhouette;
2. the gesture that touches, turns, lifts, or places it;
3. the one contextual object;
4. only then, a few internal ink marks.

At this stage, hide all hatching and interior lines. If the subject and action are unclear, do not add detail; change the crop, pose, or hero shape.

### 3. Pick one framing system

Choose exactly one per direction. Do not combine them.

| Framing | Best use | Composition rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Close service gesture | Handoff, wrapping, pouring, serving | Crop tightly around the interaction; let the hero dominate. |
| Product action | Rolling, cutting, cooling, dipping | Centre one food object; use one tool or surface as evidence. |
| Opening ritual | Turning a sign, opening a shutter, lifting a lid | Make the business cue the silhouette and the action secondary. |
| Counter vignette | Maker and product at point of sale | Keep one person and 2-3 simplified objects on one baseline. |

Never add a mascot, storefront, emblem, counter scene, and wordmark to the same illustration. Those are different framings, not ingredients to stack.

### 4. Render for line quality, then art-direct for reduction

Use a rendered ink illustration when the desired quality relies on natural contour variation, uneven hatching, and organic hand anatomy. A geometric SVG is useful later for a simplified stamp or icon, but it is not a substitute for the primary illustration.

For each render, ask for:

- black ink on a clean white field;
- a single, isolated editorial food moment;
- bold outer contour, lighter interior hatching, no tonal shading;
- 30-45% clear space around the subject;
- no border, badge, full room, or unrelated props.

Then make one reduction pass. The instruction is always subtractive: remove one prop, one texture cluster, or one secondary motion mark. Do not solve uncertainty by adding more objects.

### 5. Build the logo system after the illustration works

The illustration is the primary expression. It still needs a reduced companion for small formats.

| Deliverable | Purpose | Required reduction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary illustration | Website, packaging front, campaign, signage | Full hero + action + one context cue |
| Illustration + wordmark | Main brand lockup | Wordmark placed only after the image has a clear silhouette |
| Small mark | Avatar, stamp, receipt, favicon | Hero silhouette plus one distinguishing internal cue; remove the hand and context if needed |
| One-colour reverse | Labels and dark fields | Test figure-ground; preserve only forms that remain clear |

The small mark is derived from the primary moment. It should not introduce a second brand idea.

## Detail and craft controls

| Element | Rule |
| --- | --- |
| Palette | Ink `#111111` on white or warm paper `#F7F5EF`. No colour until the monochrome system succeeds. |
| Outer contour | Visibly stronger than internal detail. Keep the stroke family consistent across the hero, hand, and context cue. |
| Interior detail | Use it to describe material or motion, never to explain what the object is. Keep it inside the silhouette. |
| Hatching | Place in short, purposeful clusters following form. Leave at least half of the drawing quiet. |
| White space | Preserve 30-45% around the image. Empty paper is part of the mark, not unused canvas. |
| Crop | Crop to make the action immediate; do not zoom out to include the whole shop or room. |
| Typography | Hand-drawn or hand-adjusted lettering with the same rhythm as the lines. It must support the image, not compete with it. |

## The anti-messiness gate

Before refinement, answer all seven questions yes:

1. Can I name the hero object within three seconds, without reading text?
2. Is there only one visible action?
3. Does one context cue make the ritual clear?
4. Would the idea still read if all hatching and labels disappeared?
5. Is there a clear visual hierarchy: hero, action, then context?
6. Is at least one-third of the canvas deliberately empty?
7. Can the mark be reduced by removing detail rather than inventing a new symbol?

If any answer is no, return to the one-sentence moment. Do not start polishing.

## Review loop

Use this loop for every new direction:

1. **Brief:** write one sentence and identify hero, action, and context.
2. **Render:** make 2-3 variations of the same moment, changing crop or pose only.
3. **Select:** keep the variation with the fastest three-second read.
4. **Subtract:** remove one nonessential detail cluster before adding anything.
5. **Reduce:** test at 48 px and 25 mm; produce the small mark from the surviving silhouette.
6. **Lock up:** add the wordmark only once the illustration and reduced mark pass.
7. **Systemize:** use the chosen ritual as the source for packaging, signage, social, and secondary scenes.

This is the practical difference from the earlier crowded directions: do not try to prove the entire brand in a single mark. Prove one memorable moment, then let the wider system carry the rest of the world.

## Originality guardrails

The four studies are a language reference only. A new direction must change at least three of these variables:

1. food or service subject;
2. action or ritual;
3. framing system and crop;
4. business cue;
5. signature texture or graphic device;
6. wordmark placement and character.

Do not reuse an exact food pose, hand position, bag silhouette, awning geometry, or scene crop. Borrow the hierarchy and reduction discipline, not the motifs.
